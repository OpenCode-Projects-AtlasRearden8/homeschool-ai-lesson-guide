# State Paperwork — Informationally, Never Legal Advice

*Summary: how to find your own state's homeschool requirements from official
sources, organize them into your records system, and know when to ask a
professional instead of trusting a chatbot — or this guide. Time to do:
20 minutes, once, plus 10 to re-verify annually.*

## The part we have to say clearly

**This guide is not legal advice, and neither is any AI chatbot.** Homeschool
law is state-specific and changes. What this chapter does is teach you a
durable process: how to find the *official* requirements for your state,
translate them into your planner, and recognize when you're out of scope and
should contact the state department of education, a homeschool association,
or a lawyer. Everything here is educational information, like a librarian
pointing at the shelf.

## Rule one: official sources only

For legal matters, one trusted page beats a hundred summaries:

| Source | What it's worth |
|---|---|
| **Your state's Department of Education website** | Primary. Requirement text lives here or one click away. |
| **Your state's compulsory-attendance statute** (often linked from that site) | The actual law, in plain legalese. |
| **A state homeschool association's law page** | Good explanation, community-maintained. Cross-check against DOE. |
| **HSLDA or similar advocacy sites** | Useful summaries, but an advocacy org; cross-check against DOE. |
| **A blog post from 2019** | Potentially stale. Never the only source. |
| **A chatbot's answer** | A rumor with confidence. Verify against DOE before acting. |

Notice the last row. Models are trained on a web full of outdated and
conflicting homeschool law content — this exact topic is one of the highest
risk zones you could ask an AI about, because complying wrong is worse than
planning wrong.

## The seven facts to find and fill in

Open a new file — the template
[templates/state-paperwork-checklist.md](templates/state-paperwork-checklist.md)
— and find your state's answers to:

1. **Notification.** Must you tell the state/school district you're
   homeschooling? One-time, annual, or never?
2. **Who may teach.** Any parent, or a specific qualification (diploma,
   teaching license, supervision by a certified person)?
3. **Required subjects.** Some states name them (e.g., certain states require
   certain core subjects); your year plan inherits these.
4. **Required days/hours.** Attendance or hours requirements, if any.
5. **Recordkeeping.** What you must keep (attendance, test results, portfolio).
6. **Assessment.** Annual testing? Evaluation by a teacher? Nothing? Portfolio
   review?
7. **Changes or things you must do if you re-enroll** your kid in school or
   move states.

Fill it in *from the DOE page you're reading*, one row at a time. Paste short
quotes from the official page next to each row. If two sources agree, relax
and move on. If they disagree, the DOE page wins, and you note the discrepancy
in the row.

## What AI is actually useful for here

Two safe uses:

- **Simplifying language.** Paste a section of the *actual law or DOE page*
  and ask "explain this paragraph in plain English, don't add requirements."
  That's legal-language translation, not law practice — and note it still
  deserves a second read against the original, usually a recheck of
  anything surprising.
- **Building your planner around known facts.** Once you know from the DOE
  page "this state requires 180 days logged," that number becomes a
  constraint your year plan and weekly loop enforce for you (chapter 06
  notifications get easier the fewer manual chores there are).

Riskier uses to avoid entirely: "What does Indiana require?" with no source
check; "Am I legal?" questions; "Can I skip the notice?" questions. Any
time a yes/no legality answer is coming out of the model, stop, and do the
lookup yourself from the DOE site instead.

## Annual/move recheck

Set a phone calendar reminder — school start month, once a year — that says
"re-verify state homeschool requirements." One 10-minute pass through the
DOE site, comparing against your checklist file. Do it after every
inter-state move, too (requirement differences between states can be
significant).

## When to contact a professional (and where to call first)

- The DOE website genuinely can't answer your situation (custody orders,
  special-ed disputes, truancy letters already sent)
- You're navigating a disagreement with a school district official
- You need to re-enroll mid-year and the school's asking for paperwork you
  don't have

The right first calls are, in order: your **state's DOE homeschool contact**
(every DOE office has one), then a **state homeschool association** — they
deal with these situations constantly, and some offer free guidance. A
lawyer is a great resource for legal-shaped questions and worth the consult
fee for anything with custody, special-ed, or truancy stakes. Your friendly
chatbot is not on this list, and this guide isn't either.

## The one-sentence point

If the difference between "AI plausibly guessed my state's rules" and "I
copied the requirements from the DOE website myself" sounds like a big
avalanche of hassle, remember: it's a 20-minute task you do *once*, it
slides into the records tree you built in chapter 06, and it removes the
single scariest unknown in this whole project.
