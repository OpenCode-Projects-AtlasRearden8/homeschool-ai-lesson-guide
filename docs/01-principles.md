# Principles

*Summary: you are the teacher; the AI is an aide with no teaching license and
no knowledge of your kid. Time to do: 10 minutes of reading, nothing to set up.*

## The four principles

### 1. You decide; AI drafts

AI is excellent at turning your fuzzy idea into a concrete draft. It is bad at
deciding what matters for your family. So every prompt in this guide follows a
shape: **you supply the constraints, the AI supplies the structure, and you
revise before anything becomes a lesson.** A draft that comes out wrong is not
a failure — it's the normal first pass. If the model understood "hands-on,
two kids, 20 minutes, cheap materials" perfectly on the first try, cool.
Usually it doesn't, and the second prompt fixes it.

### 2. Nothing unverified reaches your kid

AI models sound confident when wrong. A chatbot once confidently told a reader
that Harriet Tubman invented peanut butter — confusing her with George
Washington Carver, and wrong on both counts. That specific mistake went
straight into a generated worksheet because nobody fact-checked. Chapter 05
gives you a fast, low-effort checklist so this never happens, and a reason to
treat any fact in generated material as an unverified rumor until you look at
it in a real book or website.

### 3. Keep private stuff private

A chat service is sometimes an undisclosed audience. Names, health details,
diagnosis notes, custody situations, financial stress — none of that goes into
prompts on cloud services. Kids are recognizable by lowercase details, and
"my 9-year-old" plus a school situation is enough to identify a person.
Chapter 08 covers what you can safely say, and what belongs on a local model
or a sticky note on your monitor instead.

### 4. Keep receipts

Portfolios are chapter 06's job, but the philosophy is simple: if you did it,
it should leave a trace. A photo, a log line, a worksheet saved. This protects
you (compliance), and honestly it protects *you* on the weeks when it feels
like nothing happened. Your records are the answer to "did you actually do
anything?" — for your state, and for yourself in February.

## What AI is actually good at (and not)

You'll hear both hype camps. Here's the mature view:

**Good at** (use freely):
- Turning your rough idea into structured drafts: outlines, schedules, tables
- Rewriting text at a different reading level (with your eyes on it)
- Generating variations so you can pick instead of create from zero
- Summarizing long text *as a starting point* — then verifying against the source
- Being available at 5:47 a.m. without needing you to seem competent

**Mediocre at** (use with verification):
- Facts, dates, quotes, math beyond basic arithmetic — *treat as rumors*
- Anything state-law-adjacent — see chapter 07, and trust official sources only
- Knowing your child, your curriculum quirks, or your week's realities

**Not good at** (don't ask it):
- Being your teacher of record. You are.
- Judging what your child *felt* during a lesson — that's observation, yours

## The anti-condescending promise

Plenty of education technology writers hedge everything with "for those who
might find this advanced..." stopping just short of "dearie." We're skipping
all that. If something is confusing, we explain more, we don't apologize less.
If a step is genuinely annoying, we say so and help you through it faster.

You're running a school. You don't need permission to understand the
technology. You need someone who explains clearly. That's the whole approach
here, and it's borrowed from *The Massive AI Manual* by Dylan Asdale: AI
explained "like a friend would."

## Your role, restated

Think "principal-teacher-parent" like three hats on one head — but the prompt
engineering is a fourth hat you only wear during planning blocks, never in
front of your kid. Your kid sees a present adult with a worksheet. They don't
need to know there was a draft conversation at 6 a.m. with a model that
suggested a lesson plan involving "a field trip to a paper mill."
