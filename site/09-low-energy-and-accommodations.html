Low Energy and Accommodations

Summary: ADHD-friendly and low-spoons adaptations for both sides of the desk — the teaching parent and the learning kid. Time to do: read now, steal what works.

This chapter exists because the whole "school at home" challenge is really an energy economy: you and your kid each have a finite daily budget, and every planning system that doesn't price that in will collapse in November. This is not a productivity lecture — the philosophy here comes from the author's own low-energy-friendly approach in The Massive AI Manual: minimize the number of decisions you make while exhausted.

For the teaching parent

Plan while fresh, execute while tired — always in that order

The 45-minute Sunday block (chapter 03) is deliberately scheduled when you have decision-making juice. The whole point is that no creative work happens on Tuesday. If your week starts on Monday, plan Sunday evening or early Monday; never plan the day of.

The two-task rule for bad days

On a genuinely awful day (sick, grief, upheaval), school is two tasks per kid, one of which is reading. Write them on a sticky note. Done means done. This is the floor — having a pre-decided floor means bad days don't trigger the "we've done nothing for a week, are we failing" spiral, and chapter 06's log line for such a day is legitimately fine: "light day: read-aloud, math review."

Pre-build a "quiet bin"

Ask the AI once — not weekly — for a list of 15 low-prep activities across your subjects: worksheets you already have, one book bin per kid, a puzzle, a documentary, an audiobook with the accompanying map. When the bad day arrives, you open the bin list, pick two, done. Ten minutes of prep in September buys you a year of grace periods.

AI as your low-energy co-planner

Three asks that earn their keep on tired weeks:

  • "Here's what we actually did this week (paste from the log). Summarize it into two portfolio sentences."
  • "Give me three 20-minute replacements for the history project we can't do, same topic, no mess, no printing."
  • "Here's my draft week. Cut it in half while keeping coverage of both required-by-us subjects." (The model is great at ruthless cutting — better than tired-you is.)

Exceptions are data, not evidence of failure

If you bailed all of week 9, that's not proof homeschooling is failing — it's a signal about load. The quarterly review (chapter 06) is where you look at the pattern with a month's distance. Ask the AI to summarize the quarter's log and name the pattern; it sees the shape better than you do from inside it.

For the learning kid

Output variety > output volume

Some kids know the material and can't produce six sentence answers about it. Alternate expected outputs around a topic: one worksheet day, one oral answers day, one drawing/diagram day, one teach-the-parent day. Ask the AI for the same content in a different output shape; that's one prompt, and it dodges handwriting-based power struggles.

Movement and choice, structured

Two cheap wins: 10 minutes of movement between subjects, and picking the order of two remaining subjects. Both cost zero planning and each one is the difference between a fight and a lesson.

When a concept isn't landing

Before the third frustrating explanation, ask the AI for alternate approaches — a story, a physical demonstration with kitchen objects, a video-style explanation, a game. Prediction: the third approach — whichever one it turns out to be — lands for more kids than grinding the first one further. (This is also one of the best uses of AI for homeschooling overall: generating six teaching angles is something no book or curriculum fits in its spine.)

Accommodations without the paperwork

You don't need a document to give your kid extended time, chunked instructions, or a quiet start. The template templates/accommodations-notes.md is a simple two-page form for what works and what doesn't — for you, and so any evaluator or future school re-entry can see the adaptations were thoughtful and consistent. Describe needs, not diagnoses (chapter 08), even in a private file, if you'd rather.

The one-line summary of the chapter

Design your school for the worst day of the month, and good days will exceed the plan — which is a much better failure mode than designing for the best day and collapsing.
