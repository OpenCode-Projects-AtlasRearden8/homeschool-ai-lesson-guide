Plan Your Year

Summary: build a 36-week plan from your subject list, your curriculum, and your real constraints — using the AI as a structuring partner, not an oracle. Time to do: about 60–90 minutes, once per year.

What a year plan is (and isn't)

A year plan is a map at the week level. Not day-by-day — that's the weekly loop's job (chapter 03). It answers: which topics, roughly which weeks, where the natural break points are, and where the padding goes when life happens.

It does not answer what your state requires. Do that lookup first — chapter 07 — because some states require specific hours, subjects, or assessments, and your year plan should inherit those constraints from the start.

Before you prompt: gather five facts

Write these down in a scratch note (or the year plan template). These are the "constraints" every good prompt in this guide assumes you gather first:

  1. Kids and rough levels. For each: age/grade-ish level and one thing you want them to gain this year. Use ordinal descriptions ("reading confidently at 3rd-grade level, resistant writer").
  2. Subject list. Both required-by-state and chosen-by-you. Separate them; some states require certain subjects explicitly.
  3. Curriculum anchors. What you already own or chose — e.g., "Saxon Math 4", "Story of the World Vol. 2", library-based science.
  4. Your calendar reality. Start date, holiday weeks, travel weeks, chairs — er, seasons you know are bad (post-illness, family emergencies). Add buffer weeks on purpose, at the end and mid-year.
  5. Your energy pattern. When do you plan? When are your kids sharp? Morning-deep or afternoon-shallow matters for schedule shape.

The year-plan prompt

The prompt library has the full version (see prompts/year-plan.md). The shape, in one paragraph:

Give the model each of the five facts above, then ask for a week-by-week table with 36 rows: week number, focus topic per subject, milestones, and buffer/assessment weeks marked. Then — this is the part people skip — ask it to identify risks: "which weeks look unrealistic for a sick day plus a toddler?" and to propose two alternative shapes (front-loaded vs. spray-across-the-year), so you pick a shape rather than accept the first one.

What a good result looks like

  • 36 rows, one per week, with one focus topic per subject per week
  • Real break weeks marked and honorable — not crumbs at the end
  • Assessment or portfolio weeks marked for those states and for chapter 06
  • A short risks section the model actually flagged (e.g., "weeks 18–19 fall during winter illness season — consider lighter load")
  • Subjects your state requires, present every week they should be

What to fix by hand, every time

The model doesn't know these things; you do, so edit them in:

  • Readers' pace. If your kid reads slower than a syllabus assumes, stretch the reading plan across more weeks. Weeks are cheap to move in the plan.
  • Bad calendar weeks. You know your family's rhythms; the model knows only what you typed.
  • Volume. If a week has three major lessons in four subjects, something is wrong for most real kids. Cut it in half. Cutting is easier than catching up.

Common failure to sidestep

The classic model answer is "180 days, 4 quarters, evenly split." Evenly split is plausible, not good. If you get that shape, ask it to redo it in the shape you actually picked (front-loaded science, seasonal history units, etc.). A second pass with explicit constraints is where plans stop looking generated.

Handoff to the weekly loop

Once the year plan exists in your own folder (Save it as year-plan.md from the template — never only inside a chat browser), each week's work is small. Open your plan, find next week's row, and pull this week's detail using the weekly loop in chapter 03. The plan never tells you how to teach Wednesday; it just makes sure Wednesday has a purpose.
