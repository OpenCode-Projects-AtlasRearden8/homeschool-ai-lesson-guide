Fact-Check Everything

Summary: AI-generated text is confident even when wrong. Here's a 5-minute per-lesson verification routine that catches almost everything, without turning you into a full-time editor. Time to do: 5 minutes per lesson.

Why this chapter exists

A model will write "Harriet Tubman developed over 300 varieties of peanuts" with the same calm sentence rhythm it uses for true things. Trusting prose rhythm is the number-one way AI errors leak into homeschool materials — because an unverified, confidently-written fact looks like every verified fact. And once it's on a worksheet, it becomes "what my mom taught me."

Two facts about the error pattern worth knowing up front:

  1. Models are worse at specifics (dates, numbers, names, quotes, statistics) than at processes and general concepts. "Why rain forms" reasoning is usually solid; the year the Kansas-Nebraska Act passed is a coin flip.
  2. Errors compound in generated material. If the model hallucinates early ("Tubman, born in 1720..."), everything after is built on it. Catch errors at generation time, not at dinner when your kid repeats them at Grammy's.

The triage table: what must be verified

Not everything needs the same care.

Content type Verify? How fast
Math arithmetic, steps ("how to regroup in column addition") Do the problems yourself 30 sec
Names, dates, inventions ("who invented the printing press") Always Book or 2 trusted sites, 2 min
Quotes ("Einstein said ...") Always — misquotes are rampant Quote is real or delete, 2 min
Statistics ("60% of farms in 1800s...") Always, and cite a source on the sheet 2 min
Definitions ("what's a serif?") Usually fine; spot-check if unusual 1 min
Step-by-step how-tos (experiment setup, craft) Check safety + physics 2 min
Discussion prompts, opinions, essay structure No — no facts to check 0 min

Two ways to verify — pick your speed

Fast path (most days). For each factual claim on the sheet: open a known reference — a real encyclopedia, your curriculum's own text, .gov or museum sites. Search "claim + correction?" doesn't work; search the claim itself, then read what a real source says. If names/dates disagree, the real source wins, every time. Then log it in your fact-checks.md (six words: "week 4, Rome dates — fixed"). Done.

Thorough path (new topic weeks). When you're teaching something well outside your own comfort zone, do the fact-check before generating materials, using the verification prompt (prompts/verification-pass.md):

  1. Ask the model to list every factual claim it used, with a source it thinks it got each from. (Models sometimes invent sources; if it can't name one, treat the claim as unsourced.)
  2. Verify each named source is real — this catches a surprisingly large share of hallucinations, since fake sources are easier to spot than fake facts.
  3. Then, and only then, generate worksheets and reading passages.

Verification helpers the model can give you

Useful, verified-safe asks:

  • "List every named entity in this text — person, place, date, number — so I can check them." (Cheap; makes the check mechanical.)
  • "What's a common misconception people have here, and is anything in this text at risk of repeating it?"
  • "Which claims here would change the lesson if they were wrong?" (Priorities your checking time.)

Not useful, and a trap:

  • "Is everything you wrote correct?" — models nearly always say yes.
  • "Search and tell me." — early tools sometimes invented search results; newer ones do real searches but grab random pages. Trust the *page you open yourself*, not the model's summary of a page you haven't seen.

The sheet-level habit

For anything that stays on a worksheet, keep one line of provenance on the page itself. A footer like: "Fact-checked against [source], [date]." Chapter 06 suggests logging these in a fact-checks.md file per unit. It takes seconds, and it's exactly the kind of habit that makes a portfolio durable if anyone ever looks closely.

Failure modes to know

  • Plausible-audio trap. A generated science explanation sounds right because it's fluent. Fluency is not evidence. Check the concept, not the vibe.
  • Outdated by design. Generated text may reflect a world that ended years ago ("free resources, current as of ..." with years you didn't mention). State laws, websites, and prices age badly.
  • Mixing up similar entities. Two people with the same fields, two treaties, two wars... this is the model's most common deep error and easy to miss because everything around it is right.

The one-line culture of this chapter: fluent text is a rumor until you've seen a source. After your first month, checking quarterly is fine — but keep the footer habit.
