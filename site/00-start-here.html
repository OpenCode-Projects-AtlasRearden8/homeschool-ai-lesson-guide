Start Here

Summary: what this guide is, what you need, and the fastest honest path through it. Time to read: 10 minutes.

Welcome. If you're opening this guide, you are doing one of the most demanding jobs that exists: being your child's teacher and their parent and the person who pays for the curriculum and the person who logs it all somewhere because somebody eventually asks.

You do not need to be great at tech for this to work. You do not need to have chosen an AI tool yet. You need about two hours and a willingness to treat the AI like a helpful, slightly too confident teaching aide — not an authority.

The one-paragraph version

You'll build three documents this month: a year plan (chapter 02), a weekly loop (chapter 03) that turns each week into about 45 minutes of planning, and a records system (chapters 06 and 07) that quietly keeps you compliant and lets you prove, months later, that learning happened. Along the way you'll learn to differentiate worksheets by reading level by hand or with AI (chapter 04), fact-check what the model tells you (chapter 05), and keep your family's data private (chapter 08).

What you need before starting

  1. One AI tool you already have access to — whether it's a free chatbot, a local model on your machine, or something paid. This guide is tool-neutral. If you haven't picked one yet, chapter 08 has a comparison.
  2. A curriculum or a rough subject list. Pick a math curriculum, a science approach, a reading plan. Even "world history + library books" counts.
  3. Your state homeschool requirements in one place. Chapter 07 walks you through where to find this officially (we don't guess for you, and no AI summarize should be trusted for this without a source — see chapter 06).
  4. One folder on your computer. Call it whatever, but this becomes your records home for the whole year. Chapter 06 gives you the structure.

The 2-hour setup path, in order

Do this Time Where
Read 01-principles.md so you know who's in charge 10 min Chapter 01
Sketch your subject list + one goal per child 15 min Chapter 02
Write a draft 36-week plan using the year-plan prompt 60 min Chapter 02
Separate "required by state" files from "for me" files 20 min Chapter 07
Set up the weekly loop folder + Sunday planning block 15 min Chapter 03

Everything after that is reusable for the whole year. Sunday evenings, one block, 45 minutes, folder of done lessons, done.

Two kinds of reading, choose yours

Read linearly (everything, in order) if you like context and want the full system before acting. That's about 3 hours and is honestly worth it once.

Grab-and-go if you'd rather fix a problem today and read the philosophy later. The chapters are written to work on their own; each one starts with a one-line summary and a time-to-do-it hint.

A promise about tone

This guide tries never to say "well, simply open the settings" about anything that confused you the first five times. If a section isn't working, that's a bug in the writing, not a verdict on your worth. Take a break, come back, and if something reads like it's talking down to you, that's a problem to report — the maintainers mean it.

The thing everyone gets wrong on day one

People open a chatbot and type "plan my homeschool year." The model, unhappily, returns something generic: 180 days, four quarters, evenly distributed, phrased like a syllabus from 2004. That's not because AI is bad — it's because that prompt has no information about your kids, your state, your energy pattern, or your curriculum.

This guide teaches you to feed the AI your context, and to keep receipts. The difference between "AI made my plan" and "I made my plan with AI helping" is basically the difference between an outline and a year you'll actually enjoy teaching.
