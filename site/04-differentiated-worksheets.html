Differentiated Worksheets

Summary: turn one lesson into three worksheets — below level, on level, above level — in about 10 minutes with a structured prompt. Time to do: 10 minutes per lesson per subject per level.

The idea in one sentence

The same material, rendered at different reading levels, so a 7-year-old and a 12-year-old — or one confident reader and one dyslexic reader — can sit at one table and work on one topic, each with a sheet that doesn't talk down to them and doesn't overwhelm them.

Reading levels, briefly

Grade levels are a shorthand, not a diagnosis. When you tell a model "3rd grade reading level," it doesn't mean the label matches your kid. So the prompt below works in descriptors, not grades — read these and pick by word count and vocabulary complexity:

Band you might say Looks like Typical features
Emerging reader read-aloud, big font, short lines 6–10 word sentences; one idea per line; no defining-vocabulary homework
Decoding comfortably short instructions, familiar words 10–14 word sentences; one style of question per section
Confident reader full sentences, richer vocab 14–20 word sentences; sequencing, why-questions
Advanced dense text, inferential work primary-source quotes, "compare," "evaluate"

The rewrite model is good at this — better than most teachers have time for — and bad at judging which band your kid is at, so that judgment stays yours.

The worksheet workflow

Step 1. Pick the lesson and the same content for all levels. If the sheet is about why leaves change color, every kid answers questions about leaves changing color — not different topics.

Step 2. Use the prompt in prompts/worksheet-three-levels.md. It takes your lesson text, the number of questions, and the level descriptors, and emits three worksheets plus a short note on what changed between them. The note matters: you should be able to glance at it and know they're the same lesson.

Step 3. Sanity-check each sheet:

  • Emerging reader sheet: Is the vocabulary actually simple? Wrong-answer section still correct? (No "ambiguously right" answers.)
  • On level: Does it ask real questions, not fill-in-the-blank trivia?
  • Above level: Does it have an inferential question (why/compare), not just harder vocabulary with the same recall answer?

Step 4. Good worksheets need good answer keys. Ask the AI for one simultaneously — it's the easiest add-on, and it's what your tired 5 p.m. self will thank you for.

Step 5 (single passages). For one-off text — a paragraph from your spine book, an explanation that landed badly, a letter for co-op adults with mixed reading levels — use the rewrite prompt instead: prompts/rewrite-reading-level.md. Same rules, one passage, plus a what-changed note you check against your original.

Formatting choices that matter more than they sound like they might

  • Font. Larger font w/ sans-serif (Arial or Verdana or APHont) helps a lot for emerging and dyslexic readers. Sites have templates; print styles matter for worksheets you'll reuse.
  • White space. Ask for "generous line spacing, no more than 5 questions per page" for the emerging band — the model tends to overpack.
  • Answer format variety. Kids hate 12 blanks in a row. Ask for one drawing task, one matching, one short sentence — variety keeps attention.
  • Avoid handwriting-heavy tasks in the emerging band unless writing IS the lesson goal. Oral answers, circling, matching, drawing.

Ethics of AI-generated kid materials

Your kid's time is your kid's real cost. Every AI worksheet that reaches them should have been either fact-checked (chapter 05) or been about opinion-free process work (math operations with correct answers you've spot-checked, handwriting, summarizing their own reading). This sounds heavy but is a quick glance — and it protects against things like the wrong kinetic-molecular explanation quietly becoming your false history of ice melting.

Sibling-proofing

One trap: generated sheets sometimes reveal the answer in a sibling's sheet level's wording. Ask, "check these three worksheets so no easy sheet contains the exact phrasing that would be an answer on the harder sheet." Cheap to request; the models catch most of it.
